```text
Operational, Managerial and Strategic Information Needs

📘 Overview
Decisions in an organization differ in who makes them, how far ahead they look and how clearly they can be described, so they need different information. Operational decisions concern day-to-day activities, managerial decisions concern monitoring performance and using resources over weeks or months, and strategic decisions concern the organization's long-term direction. Decision makers also move through stages as they find a problem, develop options, choose one and carry it out.

🧠 Key Idea
Useful information fits the decision it supports: its level of detail, time horizon, source and frequency should match what is being decided. The level of a decision and how structured it is are related but not the same, so information has to be matched to both.

⚔️ Core Details:
- Operational decisions support daily activities, such as which bus and driver will cover tomorrow's first trip for a fictional regional bus company. They need detailed, current information from inside the organization, such as bus availability and driver schedules.
- Managerial decisions monitor performance and direct resources over weeks or months, such as adjusting a route's schedule when ridership is low. They need summarized, periodic information, such as monthly ridership by route and fuel cost compared with budget.
- Strategic decisions set long-term direction, such as whether to open service in a new city. They need broader and less frequent information, including external information such as population trends, competitor fares and the expected cost of new buses.
- Decisions also differ in structure. Structured decisions can be handled with known rules, such as assigning buses by a fixed rule. Unstructured decisions have no standard procedure and rely heavily on judgment, such as deciding how to enter a new market. Semi-structured decisions have some clear rules and some judgment, such as revising schedules for a route with changing demand.
- Level and structure are related but not the same. Operational decisions are often more structured and strategic decisions often less so, but an operational response to an unusual breakdown may have no standard procedure, while a managerial decision such as approving routine maintenance spending within an agreed limit may follow clear rules.
- A model based on Herbert Simon's work describes decision making in four stages: intelligence (noticing and understanding a problem or opportunity), design (developing possible options), choice (selecting an option) and implementation (carrying it out and checking the result). Decision makers often return to an earlier stage; for example, a poor result during implementation may send them back to design new options.
- Information is used throughout the stages. The bus company's manager studies ridership records to understand a falling route (intelligence), compares possible schedules (design), selects one (choice) and tracks ridership afterward (implementation).
- Information quality matters throughout the stages. Decisions suffer when information is inaccurate, irrelevant to the decision, too late to use or hard to understand; the same ridership report that helps a manager can mislead if it is incomplete or out of date.

🎯 Why It Matters:
- Matching information to the decision avoids two problems: decision makers who lack what they need, and decision makers buried in detail they cannot use.
- Recognizing both the level and the structure of a decision helps an organization choose what information to collect and in what form, which prepares for the kinds of information systems that serve each need.
- Seeing decision making as stages that can repeat helps people notice when new information should send them back to an earlier stage.
- Linking information quality to decisions shows why information has to be judged against the decision it supports, not in the abstract.

🧠 Quick Recall:
- Operational decision — a day-to-day decision about current activities that needs detailed, current, mostly internal information.
- Managerial decision — a decision about monitoring performance and using resources over weeks or months that needs summarized, periodic information.
- Strategic decision — a long-term decision about direction that needs broader, less frequent information, often including external information.
- Structured, semi-structured and unstructured decisions — decisions that can follow known rules, that mix rules with judgment, or that have no standard procedure.
- Decision stages — intelligence, design, choice and implementation, which decision makers may revisit or repeat.
```
