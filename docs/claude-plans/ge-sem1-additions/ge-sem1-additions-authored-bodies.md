## Note 1: Film and Moving-Image Art

**Paste exactly the contents of this block (without the ``` lines):**

```text
Film and Moving-Image Art

📘 Overview
Film is a time-based art form that organizes images, sound, movement, and editing into an experience that unfolds over a set duration. Appreciating film involves analyzing how formal choices shape meaning and understanding how the conditions of production, including independent filmmaking, influence which stories reach audiences.

🧠 Key Idea
A film's meaning comes from how its images and sounds are selected and arranged, not only from its story. Independent film is often made outside large commercial studios, which can allow different subjects, styles, and voices to reach the screen.

⚔️ Core Details:
- Mise-en-scène is everything arranged in front of the camera within a shot, including setting, lighting, costume, props, and the positioning of actors.
- Cinematography shapes how a scene is seen through camera distance, angle, movement, framing, and lighting.
- Editing joins shots to create rhythm, relationships between images, and the sense of time and space.
- Sound includes dialogue, music, sound effects, and silence, and can direct emotion and attention separately from the image.
- Narrative films tell stories, documentary films present real events or subjects from a chosen perspective, and experimental films emphasize form, abstraction, or personal expression over conventional storytelling.
- Independent, or indie, films are made and often financed outside major commercial studios; festivals and digital distribution give them routes to audiences.
- In the Philippines, the Cinemalaya Philippine Independent Film Festival, first held in 2005, became a prominent platform for independent feature and short films, and Lino Brocka's Maynila: Sa mga Kuko ng Liwanag (1975), adapted from Edgardo M. Reyes's novel, is a widely studied Filipino film about urban poverty.

🎯 Why It Matters:
- A vocabulary of shots, editing, and sound lets viewers describe how a film produces its effects, instead of only saying whether they enjoyed the story.
- Knowing how films are financed and distributed helps explain why some stories and communities appear on screen while others are seldom seen.
- Films can reflect or critique the societies that produce them, so reading a film includes its historical and social setting.
- Moving-image techniques now appear in online video, advertising, and installations, so film analysis supports reading much of contemporary visual culture.

🧠 Quick Recall:
- Mise-en-scène — everything arranged in front of the camera within a shot.
- Cinematography — the use of camera distance, angle, movement, framing, and lighting.
- Editing — joining shots to control rhythm, relationships between images, and the sense of time.
- Documentary film — a film that presents real events or subjects from a chosen perspective.
- Independent film — a film made and often financed outside major commercial studios.
```

```text
Art, Popular Culture and Media in Contemporary Society

📘 Overview
Art today reaches people through popular culture, commercial design, broadcast media, and digital platforms as well as through galleries and museums. Appreciating contemporary art includes reading how widely circulated works shape tastes, identities, and economies.

🧠 Key Idea
In contemporary society a single work can act as personal expression, commercial persuasion, cultural identity, and social commentary at the same time. Asking which of these roles a work plays, and for whom, makes appreciation more precise.

⚔️ Core Details:
- Popular culture is the set of media and everyday practices that reach large audiences, such as popular music, television drama, films, games, fashion, and online memes.
- Design shapes everyday experience: packaging, logos, interface layouts, and advertisements use color, composition, and typography to attract attention and communicate a brand identity.
- Mass media distribute works at scale, so television dramas and popular songs can spread language, values, and styles across social groups.
- Digital platforms let creators publish directly to audiences, while recommendation systems influence which works people see.
- Enhanced e-books combine text with audio, video, animation, or interactive features, so reading becomes a multimedia experience.
- Creative industries, such as film, music, publishing, design, and advertising, are sectors of the economy built on artistic and cultural production.
- Popular works can reinforce stereotypes or challenge them, so critics ask who is represented, who profits, and what attitudes a work encourages.

🎯 Why It Matters:
- Treating commercial and popular forms as objects of appreciation extends critical attention from museum works to the images and sounds that shape daily life.
- Design choices influence what people notice and buy, so understanding them supports more deliberate responses.
- The economic role of cultural industries shows why copyright, fair pay for creators, and support for the arts matter to national development.
- Asking who is represented in popular media connects art appreciation to questions of identity, inclusion, and power.

🧠 Quick Recall:
- Popular culture — media and practices that reach large general audiences.
- Brand identity — the visual and verbal elements that make a product or organization recognizable.
- Enhanced e-book — a digital book that adds audio, video, animation, or interaction to text.
- Creative industries — economic sectors based on artistic and cultural production.
- Algorithmic curation — the use of recommendation systems to choose which works users see.
```

## Note 3: Global Citizenship and Global Civil Society

**Paste exactly the contents of this block (without the ``` lines):**

```text
Global Citizenship and Global Civil Society

📘 Overview
Global problems are addressed not only by governments and firms but also by civil society organizations that act across borders. Global citizenship is the idea that people have responsibilities beyond their own nation, and it raises questions about how individuals and communities can act ethically on shared concerns while staying rooted in local realities.

🧠 Key Idea
Global civil society links local experience to global action: communities, networks, and organizations pursue change through advocacy, service, and cooperation across borders. Their influence is real but uneven, shaped by funding, access, and whose voices are heard.

⚔️ Core Details:
- Civil society consists of organizations and networks that people form voluntarily outside government and business, including nonprofit groups, community organizations, advocacy coalitions, and social movements.
- Global civil society extends this activity across borders through transnational nongovernmental organizations, networks, and campaigns that coordinate action on shared concerns.
- Advocacy campaigns pursue change by raising awareness, documenting problems, lobbying governments and institutions, and supporting affected communities; examples include climate and environmental campaigns and organizations working on human rights and humanitarian aid.
- Human trafficking across borders is addressed through international agreements, such as the United Nations protocol on trafficking in persons adopted in 2000, and national laws such as the Philippines' Anti-Trafficking in Persons Act of 2003 (Republic Act No. 9208), expanded by Republic Act No. 10364 in 2012; civil society groups assist survivors and monitor enforcement.
- Appropriate technology applies science and engineering to the needs of low-income communities, such as small-scale solar power or low-cost water treatment designed around local conditions and local skills.
- Global citizenship is an ethical and civic idea, not a legal status: it emphasizes responsibility toward other people and the planet across borders, including concern for human rights, sustainability, and fairness.
- Critics note that civil society organizations differ in funding and power, may depend on donors, and may not represent the communities they speak for, so their legitimacy depends on accountability and participation.

🎯 Why It Matters:
- Seeing civil society as an actor alongside states and firms explains how issues such as climate change and trafficking gain attention and policy action.
- Case studies of communities show how people respond to the effects of globalization through organizing, advocacy, and cooperation, with differing success.
- Technology designed for poor communities shows that globalization's benefits depend on whether solutions fit local needs and resources.
- Global citizenship links personal choices and local action to global outcomes, which supports ethical reflection on responsibility and participation.

🧠 Quick Recall:
- Civil society — voluntary organizations and networks outside government and business.
- Global civil society — civil society action that coordinates across national borders.
- Advocacy — organized effort to influence policy, institutions, or public attitudes.
- Appropriate technology — technology designed to fit the needs, resources, and skills of a local community.
- Global citizenship — the idea of ethical responsibility toward people and the planet beyond one's own nation.
```
